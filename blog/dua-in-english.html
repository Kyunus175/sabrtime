Can You Make Dua in English? (Do You Have to Say It in Arabic?)

Short answer: yes, you can. Here's exactly where Arabic is actually required, where it isn't, and how to speak to Allah in your own words without second-guessing yourself.

The Short Answer

Yes — you can make dua in English, or in any language you understand. There is no requirement anywhere in the Quran or authentic Sunnah that dua must be recited in Arabic. Allah understands every language perfectly; what makes a dua meaningful is the sincerity behind it, not the vocabulary it's dressed in.

This question comes up constantly, especially for converts, second-generation Muslims who didn't grow up learning Arabic, and anyone still building their Arabic vocabulary — and the hesitation usually comes from confusing two very different things: the fixed prayer (salah) and personal supplication (dua). They follow completely different rules.

Where Arabic Is Actually Required

Arabic is required for the fixed, prescribed parts of salah — the parts with an exact wording that doesn't change:

  • Surah Al-Fatiha and any additional Quran recitation during salah
  • The takbir ("Allahu Akbar") that opens and moves between positions
  • The tasbih phrases said in ruku and sujood
  • The tashahhud recited while sitting

These stay in Arabic because they're a specific, prescribed act of worship — preserved exactly as the Prophet ﷺ performed them, the same way in every country, so that salah looks and sounds identical whether you're praying in Lagos, Jakarta, or Toronto.

Where You Can Use Any Language

Personal dua is a different category entirely, and it's flexible by design:

  • After sujood, before the tasleem — once you've said the required tasbih, you can add personal dua in English before ending the prayer.
  • After finishing salah — the dua many people make sitting after the tasleem can be entirely in your own language.
  • Outside of prayer, anytime — walking, before sleeping, during a hard moment, in the car, at 3am — dua has no fixed time, place, or language requirement outside salah itself.
Quick rule of thumb: if it's a fixed recitation that's part of salah's structure, it stays in Arabic. If it's you personally asking Allah for something, any language you're comfortable in works.

Making Dua in Your Own Words

You also don't need a memorized formula at all. Dua doesn't require polished language or a specific structure — you can simply talk to Allah the way you'd explain something to someone who already knows you completely, because He does. Many of the dua recorded from the Prophet ﷺ and the Sahabah were spontaneous, personal, and specific — not fixed recitations, but real requests, said in the moment they were needed.

If you're not sure how to start, a simple structure helps without becoming a rulebook: begin by acknowledging Allah (a phrase like "Ya Allah" or "O Allah" works fine), then say plainly what you need, in whatever words come naturally. There's no wrong way to phrase an honest request.

Why this confusion is so common

A lot of Islamic content available online only shows dua as long Arabic phrases with transliteration — which is genuinely useful for learning specific supplications, but it can quietly create the impression that dua itself has to look like that. It doesn't. Those are examples and options, not the only valid format.

Why This Question Comes Up So Often

Most of the hesitation isn't really about a rule — it's about confidence. Not knowing Arabic, or not feeling "religious enough" to phrase things the right way, can make personal dua feel like it needs permission first. It doesn't. Wudu isn't required to make dua, no specific setting is required, and no specific vocabulary is required — dua is available exactly where you are, in whatever state you're in, in the language you actually think in.

A Simple Way to Start

If English (or any language other than Arabic) is what feels natural, here's a straightforward way to begin:

  1. Start with "O Allah" or "Ya Allah"
  2. Say specifically what's on your mind — no need to generalize or formalize it
  3. Ask directly for what you need, however small or specific it feels
  4. Close however feels natural — many people end with "Ameen"

Over time, learning a few Arabic duas from the Quran and Sunnah alongside your own words is genuinely valuable — but that's an addition to personal dua, not a prerequisite for it. For a good starting point, see our guide to the meaning of common duas Muslims already say daily.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you make dua in English?
Yes. Dua can be made in English or any language you understand — there is no requirement for it to be in Arabic. Allah understands every language, and what matters most is the sincerity of the request, not the language it's made in.
Is it a sin to make dua in English instead of Arabic?
No, it is not a sin. Making dua in English or any other language is fully permissible. The only parts of the five daily prayers that must stay in Arabic are the fixed recitations, such as Surah Al-Fatiha and the tasbih phrases — personal dua made after these, or at any other time, can be in whatever language you understand.
Can I make dua in my own words?
Yes, absolutely. You don't need to recite a memorized Arabic formula — you can speak to Allah directly in your own words, in your own language, about exactly what's on your mind. Many of the Prophet's ﷺ own duas were spontaneous, personal requests rather than fixed recitations.
Can you make dua in English during sujood?
Yes. After saying the required tasbih (Subhana Rabbiyal A'la) during sujood, you may add personal dua in English or any language you understand, silently or quietly, asking Allah for whatever you need.
Do I need wudu to make dua?
No, wudu is not a requirement for making dua outside of prayer. Dua can be made at any time, in any state, in any language — while walking, before sleeping, during a difficult moment, or simply whenever you want to speak to Allah. Wudu is only required for the prayer (salah) itself.
Why do some duas have to be in Arabic?
The fixed parts of salah — Surah Al-Fatiha, the takbir, the tasbih phrases — are kept in Arabic because they are a specific act of worship with an exact prescribed form, preserved exactly as the Prophet ﷺ performed them. This is different from personal dua, which is flexible in both wording and language.

The language was never the barrier — it just felt like one. Whatever words you actually have right now are enough to start. For dua that's already written out with Arabic, transliteration, and meaning to build from, browse the rest of the SabrTime blog.
