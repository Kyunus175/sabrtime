Ayat al-Kursi: Meaning, Benefits & When to Recite It

One verse, called the greatest in the entire Quran by the Prophet ﷺ himself. Here's the full Arabic text, what it actually means line by line, and the authentic, sourced reasons Muslims recite it every day.

The Short Answer

Ayat al-Kursi is verse 255 of Surah Al-Baqarah. It describes Allah's oneness, His complete knowledge, and His authority over the heavens and the earth — and the Prophet ﷺ directly confirmed it as the greatest verse in the entire Quran (Sahih Muslim 810). It's most commonly recited before sleeping and after each of the five daily prayers, both practices carrying their own authentic hadith backing.

What Is Ayat al-Kursi?

"Ayat al-Kursi" means "The Verse of the Throne" (or footstool, depending on translation). It isn't a separate surah — it's a single verse sitting in the middle of Surah Al-Baqarah, the second and longest chapter of the Quran. It gets its name from the mention of Allah's "Kursi" within it, a word scholars have understood in different ways, though most agree it refers to something vast connected to Allah's dominion, distinct from His Throne ('Arsh) mentioned elsewhere in the Quran.

What sets this verse apart isn't length — it's density. In a handful of lines, it touches on Allah's oneness, His eternal nature, His knowledge, and His power, which is part of why it's held in such high regard.

Full Arabic Text, Transliteration & Meaning

اللَّهُ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَ الْحَيُّ الْقَيُّومُ ۚ لَا تَأْخُذُهُ سِنَةٌ وَلَا نَوْمٌ ۚ لَهُ مَا فِي السَّمَاوَاتِ وَمَا فِي الْأَرْضِ ۗ مَنْ ذَا الَّذِي يَشْفَعُ عِنْدَهُ إِلَّا بِإِذْنِهِ ۚ يَعْلَمُ مَا بَيْنَ أَيْدِيهِمْ وَمَا خَلْفَهُمْ ۖ وَلَا يُحِيطُونَ بِشَيْءٍ مِنْ عِلْمِهِ إِلَّا بِمَا شَاءَ ۚ وَسِعَ كُرْسِيُّهُ السَّمَاوَاتِ وَالْأَرْضَ ۖ وَلَا يَئُودُهُ حِفْظُهُمَا ۚ وَهُوَ الْعَلِيُّ الْعَظِيمُ
Allahu la ilaha illa huwal Hayyul Qayyum, la ta'khudhuhu sinatun wa la nawm, lahu ma fis-samawati wa ma fil-ard, man dhal-ladhi yashfa'u 'indahu illa bi'idhnih, ya'lamu ma bayna aydihim wa ma khalfahum, wa la yuhituna bishay'in min 'ilmihi illa bima sha'a, wasi'a kursiyyuhus-samawati wal-ard, wa la ya'uduhu hifdhuhuma, wa Huwal 'Aliyyul 'Adheem
Allah — there is no god but Him, the Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all existence. Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him. Everything in the heavens and everything on earth belongs to Him. Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission? He knows what lies ahead of His creation and what is behind them, and they grasp nothing of His knowledge except what He allows. His authority extends over the heavens and the earth, and safeguarding them does not tire Him. He is the Most High, the Most Great. (Surah Al-Baqarah, 2:255)

Understanding It Phrase by Phrase

Each line of this verse builds on the one before it, moving from who Allah is, to what He knows, to what He sustains:

  • "Allah — there is no god but Him" establishes pure monotheism (tawhid) as the verse's foundation before anything else is said.
  • "The Ever-Living, the Sustainer of all existence" — two of Allah's names, describing a life that never began and never ends, and a sustaining power that everything else depends on.
  • "Neither drowsiness nor sleep overtakes Him" distinguishes Allah from His creation — even the most vigilant guardian eventually tires or sleeps; Allah's awareness never lapses.
  • "Everything in the heavens and on earth belongs to Him" establishes total ownership, not just influence.
  • "Who could intercede without His permission?" closes the door on the idea that anyone — angel, prophet, or saint — can act independently of Allah's will.
  • "He knows what is ahead of them and behind them" refers to Allah's knowledge spanning past, present, and future, while creation's knowledge is limited to whatever He permits them to know.
  • "His Kursi extends over the heavens and the earth" conveys the sheer scale of Allah's dominion, and that maintaining all of it costs Him no effort or fatigue.
  • "The Most High, the Most Great" ends the verse the way it began — returning to Allah's greatness after describing His knowledge and power in between.

Why It's Called "The Greatest Verse in the Quran"

This isn't a title scholars gave it on their own — it comes directly from the Prophet ﷺ, in a conversation with his companion Ubayy ibn Ka'b:

The Prophet ﷺ asked, "O Abu al-Mundhir, do you know which verse from the Book of Allah with you is the greatest?" Ubayy answered with Ayat al-Kursi, and the Prophet ﷺ struck his chest gently and said, "By Allah, may knowledge bring you joy, O Abu al-Mundhir." — Sahih Muslim 810

Scholars point to a few reasons this verse earns that distinction: it's one of the most concentrated statements of tawhid (Allah's oneness) in the Quran, it names several of Allah's attributes in close succession, and unlike many verses, it doesn't address rulings, history, or stories — it's entirely about who Allah is.

The Key Benefits (With Sources)

A lot circulates online about this verse's virtues, so it's worth separating what's clearly and authentically established from what's less certain.

1. Protection through the night

This is the most well-known benefit, and it comes with an unusually specific backstory. Abu Hurairah was assigned to guard the Prophet's ﷺ charity storage, caught a thief three nights in a row, and each time let him go after he promised useful advice. On the third night, the thief (who turned out to be a devil) told him:

"When you go to your bed, recite Ayat al-Kursi, for then there will be a guard from Allah who will protect you all night long, and no devil will come near you until morning." — Sahih al-Bukhari 5010

When Abu Hurairah reported this to the Prophet ﷺ the next morning, he confirmed it was true — even though it had come from a devil trying to talk his way out of being caught.

2. Recitation after each obligatory prayer

Many Muslims recite Ayat al-Kursi immediately after finishing each of the five daily prayers, based on this hadith:

"Whoever recites Ayat al-Kursi immediately after each obligatory prayer, there will be nothing standing between him and entering Paradise except death." — recorded by An-Nasa'i and Ibn Hibban

Unlike the sleep hadith above, this one isn't found in Bukhari or Muslim specifically, though it's recorded in Ibn Hibban's Sahih (a respected authenticated collection) and several hadith scholars have classed its chain as reliable. It's fair to describe it as widely accepted rather than universally agreed upon at the very highest tier.

3. A commonly cited (but less firmly established) narration

You'll also come across the claim that reciting Ayat al-Kursi in a home causes Satan to leave it. This traces back to a narration through a chain that some scholars have accepted and others have been more cautious about, since it doesn't reach Bukhari or Muslim and its narrators have received mixed assessments. It's commonly repeated, but it's worth knowing it doesn't sit on the same firm footing as the sleep hadith or the greatest-verse hadith above.

When Should You Recite It?

  • Before sleeping — the most directly and strongly evidenced time, based on Sahih al-Bukhari 5010.
  • After each of the five daily prayers — widely practiced, based on the An-Nasa'i/Ibn Hibban narration above.
  • As part of morning and evening adhkar — many scholars, including Imam an-Nawawi in his book Al-Adhkar, include it among the recommended daily remembrances alongside the last three surahs of the Quran.
  • Any other time — there's no restriction on reciting it beyond these times; it can be read anytime as regular Quran recitation or a source of comfort.
A note on the bigger picture

It's easy to turn a verse like this into something recited on autopilot, especially once it's memorized. The verse itself is describing something enormous — Allah's knowledge, His power, His sustaining of everything that exists — so pausing on the meaning occasionally, even for a few seconds, tends to matter more than the repetition count.

Common Questions

Do you need wudu to recite it?

No. Reciting Quran from memory, including Ayat al-Kursi, doesn't require wudu. Wudu is only needed to physically touch a Mushaf (a physical copy of the Quran), according to the view held by the majority of scholars.

Can it be recited in English?

For it to count specifically as Quran recitation, it should be in Arabic — a translation is considered an explanation of the meaning rather than the Quran's actual wording. Reading the English meaning to understand and reflect on it, though, is valuable in its own right, and there's nothing wrong with making dua in English around it.

Is it the same as Surah Al-Baqarah?

No — Surah Al-Baqarah is the entire second chapter of the Quran, with 286 verses. Ayat al-Kursi is just one verse (255) within it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Ayat al-Kursi?
Ayat al-Kursi is verse 255 of Surah Al-Baqarah, the second and longest chapter of the Quran. It describes Allah's oneness, His complete knowledge, and His authority over everything that exists, and is widely regarded as the greatest single verse in the Quran.
Why is Ayat al-Kursi called the greatest verse in the Quran?
Because the Prophet ﷺ said so directly. In Sahih Muslim 810, he asked the companion Ubayy ibn Ka'b which verse in the Quran was greatest, and confirmed that Ubayy's answer, Ayat al-Kursi, was correct.
What are the benefits of reciting Ayat al-Kursi before sleeping?
Based on Sahih al-Bukhari 5010, reciting Ayat al-Kursi before sleep brings a guardian from Allah who protects the person through the night, and Satan will not come near them until morning.
Should you recite Ayat al-Kursi after every prayer?
It's a widely followed practice based on a hadith recorded by An-Nasa'i and Ibn Hibban, in which the Prophet said whoever recites Ayat al-Kursi after every obligatory prayer will have nothing standing between them and Paradise except death. Many scholars regard this narration as authentic, though it isn't found in Bukhari or Muslim.
Do you need wudu to recite Ayat al-Kursi?
No. Wudu is not a condition for reciting or reading the Quran from memory, including Ayat al-Kursi. Wudu is only required for physically touching a Mushaf (a physical copy of the Quran), according to the view held by the majority of scholars.
Can I recite Ayat al-Kursi in English instead of Arabic?
For it to count as reciting Quran, it should be recited in Arabic, since a translation is considered an explanation of the meaning rather than the Quran itself. Reading the English meaning to understand it, and reflecting on it in your own language, is still valuable and encouraged.
How many times should Ayat al-Kursi be recited daily?
There's no fixed numerical requirement. The two most consistently practiced times are after each of the five daily prayers and once before sleeping, which naturally adds up to six times a day if followed each time.

Eight lines, memorized by millions, recited after every prayer and before every night's sleep — Ayat al-Kursi packs more about who Allah is into a single verse than almost anywhere else in the Quran. Reading the meaning once is useful; returning to it regularly is where it tends to sink in.
