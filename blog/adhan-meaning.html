What Is the Adhan?

The Adhan (أَذَان) — literally "announcement" or "call" — is the Islamic call to prayer, recited before each of the five daily prayers to announce that the prayer time has begun. Traditionally called out by a muezzin from a mosque's minaret, it's one of the most widely recognized sounds in the world, heard in some form nearly two billion times a day across nearly every country on Earth.

Despite being heard this often, the meaning behind each phrase often goes unexamined — it becomes so familiar as a sound that the actual content, phrase by phrase, gets overlooked.

Full Arabic Text, Transliteration & Translation

اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ ، اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ
Allahu Akbar, Allahu Akbar (×4)
Allah is Greater, Allah is Greater
أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ
Ash-hadu an la ilaha illallah (×2)
I bear witness that there is no god but Allah
أَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا رَسُولُ اللَّهِ
Ash-hadu anna Muhammadar-rasulullah (×2)
I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah
حَيَّ عَلَى الصَّلَاةِ
Hayya alas-salah (×2)
Come to prayer
حَيَّ عَلَى الْفَلَاحِ
Hayya alal-falah (×2)
Come to success
اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ ، اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ
Allahu Akbar, Allahu Akbar (×1)
Allah is Greater, Allah is Greater
لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ
La ilaha illallah (×1)
There is no god but Allah

The Line Only Said at Fajr

الصَّلَاةُ خَيْرٌ مِنَ النَّوْمِ
As-salatu khayrun minan-nawm (×2)
Prayer is better than sleep

This line is inserted only in the Fajr (dawn) adhan, right after "Hayya alal-falah" and before the closing takbir. It isn't part of the adhan for Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, or Isha — it's specific to the one prayer that asks someone to leave sleep behind, addressing directly the exact resistance a person is likely to feel at that particular hour.

How to Respond When You Hear It

A common practice is to quietly repeat each phrase along with the muezzin, with two specific exceptions: during "Hayya alas-salah" and "Hayya alal-falah," instead of repeating the phrase, the listener says:

لَا حَوْلَ وَلَا قُوَّةَ إِلَّا بِاللَّهِ
La hawla wa la quwwata illa billah
There is no power or strength except with Allah

Everything else in the adhan is repeated as heard, phrase for phrase.

Adhan vs Iqama

The Adhan is the first, public call — traditionally broadcast loudly to reach as wide an area as possible — announcing that a prayer time has begun. The Iqama is a second, shorter call, recited just before the prayer itself starts, signaling to whoever is already present that it's time to line up and begin. The Iqama largely repeats the same phrases as the Adhan but shorter, without repetition, and with one addition: "Qad qamatis-salah" ("The prayer has now begun"), inserted before the closing takbir.

Why the structure builds the way it does

Notice the sequence: greatness of Allah, then testimony of faith, then an invitation to prayer, then an invitation to success — each phrase builds on the one before it. It moves from an abstract statement about Allah to a direct, personal call to action, ending on "success" rather than "prayer" — framing the prayer itself as the path to something, not the endpoint.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does Adhan mean?
Adhan (أَذَان) means "announcement" or "call," from the Arabic root meaning "to listen" or "to be informed." It refers to the Islamic call to prayer recited before each of the five daily prayers.
What is the special line only said in the Fajr adhan?
"As-salatu khayrun minan-nawm" (Prayer is better than sleep) is added twice in the Fajr (dawn) adhan only, right after the call to success. It doesn't appear in the other four daily prayer calls.
What should I say when I hear the Adhan?
A common practice is to repeat each phrase quietly after the muezzin, with two exceptions: during "Hayya alas-salah" and "Hayya alal-falah," the listener says "La hawla wa la quwwata illa billah" (There is no power or strength except with Allah) instead of repeating the phrase.
What is the difference between Adhan and Iqama?
The Adhan is the first call, announced publicly (traditionally from the minaret) to signal that a prayer time has begun. The Iqama is a second, shorter call recited just before the prayer actually starts, signaling that the congregation should line up to begin.
Why is "Allahu Akbar" repeated so many times in the Adhan?
The Adhan opens with "Allahu Akbar" (Allah is Greater) repeated four times, setting the tone for everything that follows — establishing that Allah's greatness is above every distraction the listener is about to be called away from.

Five times a day, in dozens of languages of recitation but always the same Arabic words, the same sequence repeats — greatness, testimony, invitation, success. Familiar enough to become background noise, and worth pausing on precisely because of that.
