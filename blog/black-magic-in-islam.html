Black Magic (Sihr) in Islam: What the Quran Actually Says

Sihr is treated as real in Islam, not superstition — the Prophet ﷺ himself was affected by it. Here's what the Quran and authentic hadith actually establish, and what genuine protection looks like, without the exaggerated claims that circulate online.

What Is Sihr in Islam?

Sihr — usually translated as sorcery, witchcraft, or black magic — refers to acts intended to cause harm or influence through hidden, forbidden means, typically understood as involving the aid of jinn or other than Allah. Unlike some traditions that treat magic as folklore, Islam addresses it directly as a real phenomenon in both the Quran and authentic hadith, and treats practicing it as one of the major sins.

The Quranic Basis: Harut and Marut

The clearest Quranic reference is Surah Al-Baqarah 2:102, which describes magic being taught by two angels, Harut and Marut, in Babylon — explicitly as a trial, with an explicit warning attached: they would tell anyone who came to learn from them, "We are only a trial, so do not disbelieve." The verse is clear that this knowledge could cause real harm, including separating a man from his wife, but "they harm none thereby except by Allah's permission" — magic has no independent power; whatever effect it has still operates within Allah's decree, not outside it.

When the Prophet ﷺ Himself Was Affected

One of the most direct pieces of evidence that Islam treats sihr as genuinely real: the Prophet ﷺ himself was affected by it. Aishah (RA) narrated that a Jewish man named Labid ibn al-A'sam cast a spell on the Prophet ﷺ, causing him to imagine he had done things he actually hadn't — described in the hadith as one of the most severe forms of magic (Sahih al-Bukhari 5765).

The resolution: Allah showed the Prophet ﷺ in a dream exactly where the spell was hidden — tied into knots in a well. He sent someone to retrieve it, and as the knots were undone, the effect lifted (Sahih al-Bukhari 5763). Surah Al-Falaq and Surah An-Nas are widely reported to have been revealed in connection with this incident — two short Surahs that remain the primary Quranic protection Muslims recite against harm, including sihr, to this day.

Is There "White Magic" in Islam?

No. This is one of the most common misconceptions. Islam does not recognize a category of magic that's permissible because it's framed as helpful, protective, or benevolent — "white magic" and "black magic" are both sihr, and both are forbidden. The concern isn't only the outcome (harm vs. help) but the method: sihr typically involves seeking assistance from jinn or forces other than Allah, which conflicts directly with tawhid (the oneness of Allah) regardless of the stated intention behind it.

Genuine Protection: The Three Quls

The most authenticated, specific protection comes from a hadith narrated by Abdullah ibn Khubayb (RA): the Prophet ﷺ told him to recite Surah Al-Ikhlas and Al-Mu'awwidhatayn (Al-Falaq and An-Nas) three times each, morning and evening, saying they would "suffice you against everything" (Sunan Abi Dawud 5082, Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3575, graded sahih by Al-Albani).

A Word on Symptoms and Seeking Help

Please read this section carefully. A lot of what circulates online lists vague symptoms — anxiety, nightmares, unexplained fatigue, relationship difficulties — as "signs" of black magic. Many of these same symptoms have real medical or psychological explanations that deserve proper diagnosis and treatment. Attributing them to sihr without ruling out a medical or mental health cause first can delay someone from getting help they genuinely need. If you're struggling with symptoms like these, please see a doctor first.

If, after that, you still have sincere concerns, the appropriate next step in Islam is to consult a knowledgeable, trustworthy scholar — not a self-styled "healer," spiritual practitioner, or anyone charging money for taweez, amulets, or rituals with no clear basis in the Quran and Sunnah. Legitimate ruqyah uses only Quranic verses and authentic duas, said with sincerity and full reliance on Allah — it isn't a paid service, a guaranteed formula, or a substitute for medical care.

Why I wanted to write this carefully

Searching this topic online turns up a lot of content that sounds confident but blurs together real Islamic teaching with fear-based claims and vague symptom lists. That combination can genuinely hurt someone — either by frightening them unnecessarily or by pointing them away from medical help they actually need. The Quran and Sunnah are clear enough on their own without needing to exaggerate anything.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is black magic (sihr) real according to Islam?
Yes. The Quran and authentic hadith both affirm sihr is real — it's mentioned directly in Surah Al-Baqarah 2:102, and the Prophet ﷺ himself was affected by it, as recorded in Sahih al-Bukhari 5763 and 5765. Islam takes it seriously as a real phenomenon, not superstition.
Is sihr haram in Islam?
Yes, practicing sihr is a major sin in Islam, tied to shirk (associating partners with Allah), because it typically involves seeking help from jinn or forces other than Allah. This applies regardless of whether someone frames it as "good" or "harmful" magic — Islam does not recognize a category of permissible magic.
Is there a difference between white magic and black magic in Islam?
No. Islamic teaching does not distinguish between "white" magic (claimed to help) and "black" magic (claimed to harm). Both fall under sihr, and both are forbidden, since both typically rely on means other than Allah.
What protection does Islam offer against black magic?
The core protection is reciting Surah Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq, and An-Nas three times each in the morning and evening, based on an authentic hadith (Sunan Abi Dawud 5082, Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3575). Ayat al-Kursi is also widely cited for protection, alongside regular salah, dhikr, and sincere reliance on Allah.
If I think I'm affected by black magic, what should I do?
Consult a doctor first, since many symptoms people attribute to sihr have medical or psychological explanations that deserve proper treatment. If concerns remain, speak to a knowledgeable, trustworthy Islamic scholar — not a self-styled "healer" — and rely on Quranic ruqyah rather than taweez or rituals with no basis in the Quran and Sunnah.

Real, but never beyond Allah's permission; forbidden to practice, and answered — not with fear or a paid formula, but with the same short Surahs that were revealed for exactly this purpose.
