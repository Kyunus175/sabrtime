Missed Prayers (Qaza): Full Guide & How to Estimate the Count

Years of missed prayers can feel too large a number to face. Here's how Qaza actually works, the order it needs to follow, and a simple calculator to turn a vague worry into an honest, workable estimate.

What Is Qaza Prayer?

Qaza refers to an obligatory prayer performed after its designated time window has passed. Islam treats the five daily prayers as a standing obligation — missing the window doesn't erase the obligation, it just means the prayer is now owed as Qaza rather than performed as Ada (on time). This applies whether a prayer was missed for a valid reason (illness, travel, forgetfulness) or through simple negligence; either way, the missed prayer still needs to eventually be made up.

The Quran describes prayer as having fixed times: "Indeed, prayer has been decreed upon the believers a decree of specified times" (Quran 4:103). That structure is exactly why a missed prayer doesn't simply disappear — the obligation was tied to a time, and when that time passes unfulfilled, the obligation carries forward rather than expiring.

Common myth: "It's been too long, so it doesn't count anymore." There's no expiry window on Qaza. Whether a prayer was missed last week or twenty years ago, the obligation to make it up remains exactly the same — time passing doesn't reduce or cancel it. This is precisely why an honest estimate, rather than an exact record, is the accepted starting point (see the calculator below).

How to Pray Qaza — Step by Step

A Qaza prayer follows the same steps as the original obligatory prayer it replaces — same number of rakahs, same recitations, same physical movements. The differences are only in timing:

1. Make wudu, exactly as for any obligatory prayer — there's no exemption from this.

2. Set the intention specifically for the missed prayer — for example, "I intend to pray the Qaza of today's Dhuhr" (or, if the exact date is unknown, simply "the earliest Dhuhr I owe").

3. Pray it exactly as you would the on-time version — same rakahs, same structure.

4. Qaza can generally be prayed at any time of day, with the same narrow exceptions as voluntary prayers — avoid the specific windows at sunrise, solar noon, and sunset.

Does the Order Matter?

In the Hanafi school, maintaining the original sequence (tarteeb) — praying missed prayers in the order they were originally due — is only required when six or fewer prayers are outstanding. Once the backlog grows beyond that, strict sequence is no longer obligatory. This isn't a loophole; it's a deliberate accommodation, because requiring rigid order across years of missed prayers would make catching up needlessly difficult for someone with a large backlog to work through.

Why this matters practically

This rule exists specifically to prevent someone with years of Qaza from feeling that they must reconstruct an impossible, exact chronological record before they can even begin. Once the count is large, you can simply start making them up — order stops being the obstacle.

A Sustainable Pace, Not a Sprint

Trying to clear years of Qaza in a burst of motivation over a few weeks rarely holds up, for the same reason most ambitious habit changes don't — see our note on why habits that survive are built small, not intense. A more sustainable approach many people use:

  • Stabilize first. Get all five daily prayers happening consistently before adding Qaza on top.
  • Attach one Qaza to each obligatory prayer. Praying one extra Qaza prayer alongside each of the five daily prayers adds five Qaza prayers a day without requiring a separate block of time.
  • Or block a weekly session. Some people prefer the opposite approach — dedicating a consistent hour or two once a week (a Sunday morning, for instance) to a focused run of Qaza prayers, rather than spreading them across every prayer. Either method works; use whichever one you'll actually keep doing.
  • Increase gradually if it's sustainable. Once the pace feels manageable, adding a second Qaza per session compounds the pace without risking burnout.

At even a modest, steady pace, a backlog that once felt permanent becomes a number that's actually closing.

Beyond the Obligation

Making up missed prayers isn't only about clearing a debt. People who work through a Qaza backlog consistently often describe a few things happening alongside it: a renewed sense of connection after each one is completed, better time-management habits spilling into on-time prayer generally, and a kind of humility that comes from actively addressing past shortcomings rather than carrying vague guilt about them indefinitely. The process tends to reinforce itself — each completed Qaza prayer makes the next one feel more achievable, not less.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Qaza prayer?
Qaza refers to an obligatory prayer made up after its designated time has passed. Islam requires missed obligatory prayers to eventually be made up rather than simply skipped, whether they were missed due to a valid excuse or through negligence.
Do I need wudu for Qaza prayer?
Yes — Qaza prayers require the same wudu (ablution) as any obligatory prayer. There is no exemption from this requirement.
Can I pray Qaza at any time of day?
Generally yes, with the same exceptions that apply to voluntary prayers — avoiding the specific windows at sunrise, solar noon, and sunset. Outside those windows, Qaza can be prayed any time, including back-to-back with other Qaza prayers.
Do I have to pray Qaza prayers in the original order?
In the Hanafi school, maintaining the original sequence (tarteeb) is required only when six or fewer prayers are owed. Once more than that are outstanding, strict sequence is no longer obligatory, which prevents the requirement from becoming overwhelming for someone with a large backlog.
How do I estimate how many prayers I've missed?
A common approach is to count the days between reaching puberty and becoming consistent in prayer, multiply by 5, and subtract any valid exemption periods (such as menstruation for women). Since exact records are rarely available, a reasonable, honest estimate is accepted rather than requiring perfect precision — Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity (Quran 2:286).
Can I pray extra Sunnah or Nafl instead of making up Qaza?
No — voluntary prayers don't substitute for missed obligatory ones. They're valuable in their own right, but Qaza specifically requires making up the missed fard prayer itself; extra Sunnah prayed alongside it is a bonus, not a replacement.
Is there a reward for making up missed prayers?
Yes — fulfilling Qaza is considered discharging a genuine religious obligation, and scholars generally encourage treating it with the same seriousness as any fard prayer, without needing to feel that the moment has permanently passed.

A large Qaza count is common, and it isn't a permanent mark against anyone — it's simply an obligation that's still open, with a clear, workable way to close it. The number matters less than starting.
