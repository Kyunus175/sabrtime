The Exact Angle

From Dallas, Texas, the Qibla bearing is approximately 43.5° from true north — calculated using the great-circle path from Dallas's coordinates to the Kaaba's fixed location at 21.4225°N, 39.8262°E. On a standard compass, that places it solidly in the northeast sector, not due east.

Rule of thumb: if you're anywhere in the Dallas–Fort Worth area, a bearing close to 43-44° will be accurate. For an exact reading at your specific address, a live GPS-based Qibla compass is more precise than relying on this citywide average.

Why Northeast, Not East

This surprises a lot of people the first time they check. Dallas sits west of Makkah on a flat map, so the intuitive guess is to face roughly east. But the actual shortest path between two points on a sphere — a great-circle route — very often doesn't match a straight line drawn on a flat map. For most of North America, including Dallas, that shortest path curves north, which is why the real bearing lands in the northeast rather than due east.

A useful way to picture this: think of a commercial flight from Dallas heading toward the Middle East. Airlines don't fly a straight eastward line across the Atlantic — they route north, often passing near Canada, Greenland, or Northern Europe, before curving southeast toward their destination. That's not a quirk of air traffic routing; it's genuinely the shortest distance across a curved surface. Qibla direction follows the exact same geometry, just measured as a fixed bearing rather than a flight path.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming "Texas is south, so Qibla must involve south." Texas's own position within the US doesn't change the underlying geometry — the bearing is calculated from your exact coordinates to Makkah's, not from any regional assumption.
  • Trusting an old compass reading from a different address. Moving even a few miles across the metro changes the bearing slightly; recalculating after a move, rather than reusing an old number, keeps the reading accurate.
  • Ignoring magnetic interference indoors. Structural steel, electronics, and speakers can throw off a magnetic compass reading by several degrees — a GPS-based Qibla app avoids this problem since it calculates from location, not a magnetic sensor.

How to Find It Accurately in Dallas
