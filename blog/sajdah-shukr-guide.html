Sajdah Shukr: The Prostration of Gratitude — Full Guide

Good news arrives, and the instinct to drop into sujud right there is a real, recorded practice — but ask four scholars whether you should, and you'll get more than one answer.

What Is Sajdah Shukr?

Sajdah Shukr — the "prostration of gratitude" — is a single prostration made outside of formal prayer, in direct response to receiving good news or being spared from harm. It's spontaneous by nature: there's no scheduled time for it, no fixed occasion that requires it. It happens in the moment relief or good news arrives, as an immediate physical expression of thanks rather than a planned act of worship.

It's performed the same way as Sajdah Tilawah — a single prostration, no tashahhud, no salam — but the trigger is entirely different. Sajdah Tilawah responds to a specific verse of the Quran; Sajdah Shukr responds to a moment in your own life.

The Evidence: When the Prophet ﷺ Did It

Authentic reports describe the Prophet ﷺ prostrating in gratitude on specific occasions when good news reached him — a moment of relief, a piece of welcome news arriving unexpectedly. These accounts describe it as occasional and situational, tied to a real moment, not a regular practice woven into daily worship the way the five daily prayers are.

Worth noting: the reports describe this happening rarely, in response to genuinely significant news — not as a habit performed for every small piece of good fortune throughout an ordinary day.

What the Four Madhabs Actually Say

This is the part most guides skip past, and it's genuinely more interesting than a simple "yes, do this." The four schools of Islamic jurisprudence don't fully agree:

  • Shafi'i and Hanbali: both consider Sajdah Shukr a Sunnah — recommended and rewarded when the occasion arises.
  • Hanafi: genuinely split, even within the school itself. Imam Abu Hanifa held that the prostration is disliked and not rewarded, reasoning that if it were encouraged every time a favor from Allah was received, a person would be prostrating constantly, since Allah's favors are continuous. His two most senior students, Abu Yusuf and Imam Muhammad, disagreed — they held it to be a means of drawing closer to Allah and rewarded, following the same method as Sajdah Tilawah.
  • Maliki: Imam Malik considered it disliked (makruh), though some later Maliki scholars permitted it. A number of Maliki scholars instead recommend two rakahs of voluntary prayer as the way to express thanks for a favor, rather than a single prostration.
Why this disagreement isn't a problem

This kind of difference of opinion is common across many secondary matters in fiqh, and it doesn't mean one school is "wrong." If you follow a specific madhab, following that school's position on Sajdah Shukr — whether that means prostrating, praying two rakahs, or simply making dua — is the straightforward approach. If you don't follow a specific school closely, the Shafi'i and Hanbali position (and the view of Abu Yusuf and Muhammad within the Hanafi school) gives clear grounds to perform it.

How to Perform Sajdah Shukr

Where it's practiced, the method mirrors Sajdah Tilawah exactly:

1. Face the Qiblah if reasonably possible.

2. Say "Allahu Akbar" and go into a single prostration.

3. Recite "Subhana Rabbiyal A'la" (Glory be to my Lord, the Most High), and add personal words of gratitude if you wish — in Arabic or your own language.

4. Rise, saying "Allahu Akbar" again.

That's the complete act — no tashahhud, no salam, no additional rakah. If you're in a public place where prostrating would draw unwanted attention, delaying it briefly to a more private moment, or simply making dua of thanks instead, is a reasonable accommodation.

When It Applies — and When It Doesn't

Common examples people describe using it for: news of a safe birth, an unexpected recovery from illness, a narrowly avoided accident, or significant relief after prolonged hardship. It isn't intended as a response to every minor pleasant moment in a day — the reports describing the Prophet ﷺ doing this point to genuinely notable occasions, not routine ones.

If a congregational prayer is happening nearby, or the moment isn't appropriate for prostrating (a Makrooh time, an unclean location), a spoken dua of gratitude is a perfectly valid substitute in the moment, with the prostration performed later if still meaningful to do.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Sajdah Shukr?
Sajdah Shukr, the "prostration of gratitude," is a single prostration made outside of formal prayer upon receiving good news or being spared from harm, as a spontaneous expression of thanks to Allah. It's performed the same way as Sajdah Tilawah — one prostration, no tashahhud or salam.
Do all four madhabs agree Sajdah Shukr should be done?
No — this is genuinely disputed. The Shafi'i and Hanbali schools consider it a Sunnah. Within the Hanafi school, Imam Abu Hanifa himself considered it disliked, while his two senior students, Abu Yusuf and Muhammad, held it to be encouraged and rewarded. Some Maliki scholars considered it disliked, while others permitted it, and some recommended two rakahs of prayer instead.
Do I need wudu for Sajdah Shukr?
The majority view is yes, the same as for Sajdah Tilawah, since it's an act of worship similar to prayer. Some scholars have permitted it without wudu given its spontaneous nature, but maintaining wudu is the safer and more commonly held position.
What do I say during Sajdah Shukr?
The same tasbih as regular sujood is sufficient — "Subhana Rabbiyal A'la" (Glory be to my Lord, the Most High) — followed by any personal words of gratitude. There's no fixed, required wording beyond the tasbih itself.
When did the Prophet ﷺ perform Sajdah Shukr?
Authentic reports describe the Prophet ﷺ prostrating in gratitude upon receiving specific pieces of good news — this was occasional, tied to a specific moment of relief or good news, not a regular or scheduled practice.

Three kinds of sujud live outside the structure of ordinary salah — one for a verse, one for a mistake, and this one for a moment of relief that felt too big to just say "Alhamdulillah" and move on. Whichever position your madhab holds, the instinct behind it is the same: gratitude that needs somewhere physical to go.
